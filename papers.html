The Arab Slave Trade and the Diffusion of Islam in Africa

with Lydia Assouad and Giulia Buccione. Presented at the NBER Economics of Religion Conference, November 2026.

We show that the Arab slave trade spread Islam in Africa through an incentive in Quranic law to convert to avoid enslavement, and that this origin shaped the norms the religion took on among exposed groups. Because exposure to raiding was not random, we use geographic and historical features that limited cavalry raiding, such as terrain variability and disease environments, as instruments for exposure across Murdock homelands. We link this variation to folklore catalogues and to present-day survey microdata from the DHS and Pew to trace both the spread of Islam and the form it took.

Borrowed Ground: Land Sharing as Insurance against Drought in Rural Tanzania

MSc dissertation, London School of Economics, 2026.

The risk-sharing literature focuses on cash and food transfers, which break down under covariate shocks like drought because everyone is hit at once. I argue that land is different: land-rich households lose little immediate consumption by lending peripheral plots. Using three waves of Tanzania's National Panel Survey and gridded rainfall data, I treat rent-free land lending, usually seen as a symptom of incomplete land markets, as an insurance mechanism. Land sharing expands under rainfall shocks as the uncultivated and peripheral plots of land-rich households flow toward the most vulnerable households in each community, almost always within networks of close kin and friends.
